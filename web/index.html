Each machine keeps everything about it: its size, options and table, filaments and saved presets, As built, the scene, the spoilboard's cuts and its own SD card and Flash (config.yaml), its front plate design. The theme, the page layout, sound and your part files are shared by all of them. Pick one to switch to it (the page reloads).

New starts from the defaults. Duplicate copies this one, its SD card, Flash and spoilboard included. Rename and Delete act on the one you're on; Delete asks first, and the last one can't go.

Export saves this machine as a .lr4.json file: its settings always; its SD card + Flash files if ticked (they make the file bigger); its front plate design always. The spoilboard's cuts stay in this browser. Import adds a machine from such a file and switches to it.

A link someone sent you (Configure > Copy a link) opens as a shared build: look and change all you like, nothing of yours changes. Save as one of my machines keeps it; Back returns to yours.

The machine

Ideal: a perfect machine - square, level, flat and trammed.

Real-world: like a real LR4, a little out of square until it homes, with a warped spoilboard and the router maybe out of tram. Run shows how far out it is, with Power cycle and a squareness test. Set how far out under Errors.

Homing fixes

Each Y and Z motor has its own switch: $H pulls each side to its switch and off by its own pulloff_mm, which squares the gantry and levels the beam.

Stops apart: how far the Max side's switch trips past the Min side's (empty = what your pulloffs make up for).

motor0: which end the Y0 / Z0 motors and switches are on. V1E's docs: "Typically, as you face the machine, the left side is Y0 and Z0" (the Min end); some machines are wired the other way. Changing it restarts FluidNC.

Rack / Beam tilt up to: how far out each side lands at power-up, when the motors let go.

Homing doesn't fix

A warped spoilboard: Warp X = the +X end higher than the −X end, Warp Y = the back higher than the front, Bow = the middle above the edges (negative = dished). Changing it lays a new spoilboard (asked first if it has cuts); surfacing flattens it.

A router out of tram: the bit's bottom tilted down toward +X / +Y. It leaves lines when surfacing; shim the router mount.

Lost steps

A stepper slips (loses steps, with a thunk) when the cut asks more of it than it has: too deep or too fast for the bit, the router off, a dull bit. FluidNC counts the steps it sent, so it never knows: the rest of the job runs shifted by what the motor lost. $H finds where the machine really is.

Drag: how hard each axis is to move, 0% = clean and lubed, 100% = dry and gritty. Z slips most: dry lead screws waste most of the motors' push, and a plunge adds to it. X has one motor; Y has two, and the one nearer the router takes more of the cut, so it slips first (the gantry soon pulls the other along: it stays nearly square).

The numbers are illustrative (a NEMA17 at V1E's 0.8 A, a typical cutting force for MDF), not measured.

Heat

A bit makes heat where it rubs instead of cutting: sitting still with the router on, a feed too slow for the rpm (the chipload, feed ÷ (rpm × flutes), under about 0.025 mm), a dull bit. Proper chips carry the heat away. The hot spot smokes, then scorches, and wood can catch fire.

Heat: smoke, scorch marks and a warning on a FluidNC Run. Fire: it can catch (the help videos use it), and the shop's extinguisher comes over by itself and puts it out. A real fire doesn't wait: never leave a running router, and keep an extinguisher in reach.

The numbers are illustrative: a dwell smokes in about 5 s and would catch in about 15 s.

A rack or beam tilt of a few tenths of a mm can't be seen at actual size. This draws the gantry and beam that many times further out of square so you can see which way it's off.

Drawing only: the cuts, the readouts and the G-code always use the real values.

Front plate design: none

Your own design engraved, pocketed or cut into the front strut plate (the one you see). It goes into the strut plate G-code (Download G-code, and the job on the SD card) and shows on the plate in the view.

Download the template: an SVG for this machine's plate length, to draw on in Inkscape. Its layers: Plate (guide) is locked: the outline, in red where nothing can go (the screw heads and the edge), and in gray the X belt, which runs in front of the plate: shapes there are cut but hidden once it's on the machine. Engrave: lines a V-bit follows. Pocket: closed shapes cleared with the 1/8" bit to the pocket depth (a layer named like "Pocket 2mm" sets its own). Cut through: closed shapes cut right out, inside the line (long ones keep 2 small tabs). Notes is ignored. Example is a hidden sample: show it to try it. Without layers, the line colour picks: black engrave, blue pocket, red cut through.

Upload a design: the SVG back. Text has to be turned into paths first (Inkscape: Path > Object to Path). The list under the buttons says what's wrong, if anything; a design with problems isn't cut or shown.

Engrave depth and Pocket depth in mm; V-bit: its angle, 60° or 90°. Pockets: the strut job's 1/8" only (no extra bit change), or 1/8" + 1/16" clean-up: the 1/8" clears what it fits and a 1/16" (one more bit change, T3, 0.75 mm a pass) cleans up only what's left - narrow strokes and inside corners. Cut-throughs always use the 1/8".

The job then starts with the V-bit (T2): zero, engrave; with the 1/16" clean-up it stops for that (T3) and does the bits the 1/8" can't reach; then it stops for the 1/8" bit, zeroes Z again and cuts the plates as usual.

A design fits one plate length: after a size change it's set aside until you upload one made from the new template. It's kept with this machine (My machines: Duplicate and Export carry it; share links don't).

    The job changes bits here. Set the bit that goes in now - the file's own (ToolDef …) fills it in when it has one - put it in the router, then resume.

    Bit: flat end mill, ball end, bullnose, V-bit, chamfer or tapered ball. Diameter in mm (the cutting diameter). Angle: V-bit / chamfer / tapered ball, tip to tip. Tip: the flat at a V-bit's or chamfer's point, or a tapered ball's ball diameter. Corner radius: a bullnose's. Flutes: how many (0 = not said). Sticks out: mm below the collet, for the drawing only (0 = as the model's bit). The router's collet takes 1/4" shanks at most, so a bit wider than that is drawn on a 1/4" shank.

    FluidNC, the real firmware, runs in your browser and drives the machine with the config.yaml in use (V1E's JP3_LR until you load your own, under Configure > 6 Get the files): home it, jog it, zero it, run files from its SD card, or type G-code in the console. It runs a copy with plain stepper drivers ($CD shows that copy).

    Click a Work or Machine value to type where to go: Enter sends G90 G0 (work) or G53 G0 (machine). Zero sets work zero where the bit is (G10 L20 P1). Feed and Rapid are FluidNC's realtime overrides. 👁 on a file previews it fast without moving the machine.

    The switch panel is on the table's front edge, at the other end from the pendant. Click its parts in the view, or use these buttons.

    E-STOP (the red mushroom) cuts the power to everything. FluidNC stops where it is and the motors go limp, so it no longer knows where the machine is. The router and the vac spin down, and the bit cools. A fire keeps burning: the e-stop only takes away the power. The green lamp is off while it's pressed.

    Release it with a quarter twist. The power comes back and FluidNC restarts. Home it before the next job.

    Router and Vac are lighted rockers: lit means on, with power. The router turns only when the job turns it on, and the vac runs with it. The router also has its own switch on its body. Click it on the model to turn it off: the router won't start until it's back on.

    On the table

    What's on the machine's table and in its router, as in a real shop. Everything that moves the bit cuts what's here: jobs, jogs, the console, the pendant, a web UI.

    Set it up by hand here, or let a job bring its own: a file with a (Stock: …) line lays that stock down, and its (ToolDef …) lines name its bits. A file without them uses the stock and bit set here.

    Touch plate for zeroing Z, Stock = the board you cut, Bit = what's in the router, Spoilboard = the table's top, which keeps every cut that goes too deep. The ? on each explains it.

    A 0.5 mm metal plate wired to FluidNC's probe input, its clip on the router's collet nut, as the real one. Touch plate under the bit lays it on whatever is under the bit now: the stock, else the spoilboard.

    Then probe from the console, e.g. G38.2 Z-25 F100: the bit comes down and stops the moment it touches the plate. G10 L20 P1 Z0.5 then makes the plate's top Z 0.5, so the stock's top is Z 0. Put the touch plate away before cutting.

    Jobs that probe (a G38.2 in the file, like the strut plates) lay their own plate when they get there and take it away after; you needn't do this for them.
